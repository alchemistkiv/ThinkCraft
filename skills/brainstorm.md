# brainstorm

## Goal

Generate multiple directions, approaches, hypotheses, and candidate solutions around a topic at speed.

## When to use

- The problem space is defined narrowly, but the solution space has not been opened yet.
- Early-stage work needs to expose more than one idea direction.
- There is a risk of locking onto a single solution too early.
- Creative but technically meaningful alternatives are needed.

## Inputs

- Topic or problem statement
- Goal or desired outcome
- Known constraints
- Existing ideas or rejected directions, if any

## Process

1. Lead with the point: state the problem frame in one sentence and separate the goal from the success direction.
2. Split fixed constraints, changeable assumptions, and unexplored space so the idea search stays grounded.
3. Choose at least three genuinely different idea axes; do not count variations of the same idea as separate axes.
4. Generate two to four concrete candidates under each axis; say in one line what need each candidate answers.
5. Remove repeated, renamed, or context-misaligned candidates, then group the remaining set by theme.
6. Build a shortlist of two to four candidates worth carrying forward; give each a quick score on `novelty`, `feasibility`, and `expected upside` using a light `1-5` scale.
7. Carry forward the best-ranked candidates and add one short reason each, using plain language rather than report-style framing.

## Output shape

- Short problem frame
- Grouped idea list
- One-line rationale or potential advantage for each idea
- Shortlist with quick scores
- Two to four candidates that should be analyzed next

## Guardrails

- Write like a sharp teammate, not a consultant.
- Lead with the point; do not spend lines on ceremonial setup.
- Keep structure light unless heavier formatting helps comparison.
- Avoid obvious restatement, management-speak, and padded transitions.
- Do not close too early on the first idea that appears; keep the solution space open long enough.
- Do not count renamed variations as new ideas.
- Do not include candidates that break constraints, disconnect from inputs, or are entirely impractical.
- Do not leave ideas as scattered notes; group them by axis or theme.
- Do not promote next-step candidates without rationale or light scoring; show why each was elevated.
- Do not turn brainstorming into decision-making; if a choice is required, hand off to `decide` or `analyze`.

## Handoff

If ambiguity remains high, use `clarify` to narrow the problem. If promising candidates exist, move to `analyze` for constraints, risks, and fit.
